## Decisions and additions

All 35 existing notes were substantively revised: **23 fundamental**, **12 interest**. Each retains its stable note ID and original anchor, with a specific editorial reason. Existing note prose fell from approximately **4,617 to 2,611 words**, a reduction of about **43%**, including abbreviated citations. Each revision retains one concrete, sourced interpretation rather than mechanically trimming sentences.

Three existing connections were revised: `david-and-the-enduring-kingdom` and `hannah-and-marys-song` are fundamental; `deep-abigail-wisdom-and-peace` is interest. Original endpoints and source links are preserved. No introduction changes were needed for this assignment.

One new fundamental note:

- **1 Samuel 22:17, “Obedience has a moral limit”** (`FINAL-SAMUEL-001`). The guards' refusal to kill the priests gives a concrete biblical setting for CCC 2242's requirement to refuse civil commands contrary to moral order, fundamental rights or the Gospel. The unique exact anchor “wouldn’t put out their hand” has no existing-note overlap. The note does not imply that refusal spared the victims: Doeg subsequently carried out the violence.

One new fundamental connection:

- **1 Samuel 21:4–6 / Matthew 12:3–8, “Holy bread and the claims of mercy”** (`final-holy-bread-and-mercy`). Jesus explicitly recalls the holy bread given to David. Chrysostom, *Homilies on Matthew* 39.1–3, connects the priest's provision, relief of hunger, the disciples' innocence and Christ's authority. It also distinguishes genuine necessity from using desire to excuse murder or adultery. This is a direct scriptural-reference connection, distinct from root's broader Eucharistic synthesis. The Gospel reviewer confirmed no competing holy-bread link.

## Significant cuts and corrections

- Hannah's anguish is recognised as prayer, while Eli's first judgement is corrected. Samuel's listening leads to speaking difficult truth to the same imperfect guide who helped him.
- Eli's failure is more accurately described as a rebuke that did not become effective restraint. Family or institutional loyalty is not treated as an excuse for exposing others to abuse.
- The ark's holiness is distinguished from attempts to use it as a guarantee of victory. Dagon's fall retains the point that God's freedom is not measured by Israel's military fortunes.
- Royal anointing, virtue and charisma are distinguished. David's election does not guarantee every later choice, nor does his importance in salvation history excuse the harm he does.
- **1 Samuel 15:22** now names the grave interpretive difficulty of the command to destroy a population and adds exact **CCC 2313** evidence: respect for noncombatants and condemnation of extermination. The earlier vague warfare caveat was insufficient. Coordinated with the Joshua reviewer; neither audit invents a harmless historical reconstruction or pretends this moral boundary fully resolves the ancient text.
- Ambrose's account of fortitude explicitly requires justice. His praise of David's battle courage is not made a general permission to attach God's name to one's own cause.
- David's restraint towards Saul includes speaking against the injustice and keeping his distance. The text cannot be used to protect abusive officeholders from accountability.
- Jonathan's covenant and David's continuing kindness to Mephibosheth retain concrete obligations, material security and belonging. Mephibosheth is not reduced to his disability.
- Augustine's interpretation of the Davidic promise is identified as a Christian reading of fulfilment. Royal adoptive sonship and Christ's unique divine sonship remain distinct; the historical king's possible iniquity is not attributed to Jesus. Augustine's anti-Jewish polemic is excluded from the retained explanation.
- Bathsheba's largely unreported perspective is not invented; the king's initiative and power imbalance remain explicit. Tamar's resistance, injury and lack of narrated justice remain central rather than becoming mere background for rivalry between men.
- David's confession does not erase injured people. The child's subsequent death is acknowledged as troubling and is not turned into a rule that a child's illness or death proves parental guilt.
- Ambrose's praise of David's mourning for Saul and Jonathan is explicitly a comparison when applied to Absalom; the source is not falsely presented as direct commentary on Absalom's death. Bereaved parents are not invited to condemn themselves for surviving.
- David's costly offering is explained through fair dealing, not through a claim that suffering or higher expenditure makes worship more acceptable.

## Catechism-theme gaps and rejected candidates

These are meaningful remaining limits, not a proposal to fill every chapter.

| Priority | Concrete passage | Remaining interpretive need / decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| High | 1 Samuel 15:3; 27:8–11; 2 Samuel 8:2 | Commands and acts of killing noncombatants cannot be sanitised by unsupported hyperbole or made a moral rule. CCC 2313 now supplies an explicit boundary at 15:22; the historical and theological difficulties remain. |
| High | 2 Samuel 12:14–23 | The child's death calls for a substantial treatment of innocent suffering and divine judgement. The existing confession note now prevents a general diagnosis of parental guilt, but does not claim a complete theodicy. |
| Medium | 1 Samuel 16:14–23 | “Evil spirit from the Lord” needs careful treatment of providence, moral evil and the text's ancient language. No retrospective psychiatric diagnosis or equation of mental illness with demonic activity was added. |
| Medium | 2 Samuel 6:6–9 | Uzzah's death remains difficult. General teaching about reverence does not by itself explain the severity; no invented motive was assigned to him. |
| Medium | 2 Samuel 24:1 / 1 Chronicles 21:1 | The differing presentation of incitement and David's responsibility warrants direct source interpretation. No claim that God authors moral evil, or that a census is inherently sinful, was added. |
| Medium | 2 Samuel 21:1–14 | The executed descendants and Rizpah's bodily care raise issues of responsibility, justice and burial. A generic works-of-mercy note would leave the harder context unexplained; no addition was made merely to highlight an otherwise uncovered chapter. |
| Low | 1 Samuel 14:24–45 | Saul's rash oath and the people's rescue of Jonathan could illuminate the moral limits of promises. The new 22:17 note already supplies the stronger authority/conscience case. |
| Low | 2 Samuel 23:15–17 | David refuses water won at others' mortal risk. Useful alongside 24:24, but the existing offering note already addresses shifting costs onto others. |

The account of Saul's death and the Amalekite's later report are not forced into a reconstruction beyond what the texts say. No claim is made that the deceased have a known final spiritual fate. No numerical quota drove additions.
